One degree matters a lot. Going from 25 to 26 °C drops label compliance from 98% to 47%, and going from 30 to 31 °C drops it to 2%. For anyone monitoring a warehouse, a delivery van or a pharmacy shelf, that is where the alerts should be.

Finding 03 · Newer labels are colder and more specific

How label language is changing

We grouped labels by the date of their current version. Recently revised labels are more likely to need cold storage and more likely to spell out temperature excursions.

Finding 04 · Temperature is only part of it

The fine print

Almost every label (98%) adds at least one handling instruction on top of the temperature. Switch the view to see how refrigerated products differ.

View as table

Refrigerated labels are about 7× as likely to say “do not freeze” and 11× as likely to include a “discard after” deadline. A single temperature column would miss both.

Finding 05 · Cold-chain products need more handling

Refrigerated labels ask for more

Compared with room-temperature products, refrigerated products have longer storage sections, more rules and more warnings. After leaving the fridge, the most common deadline is 30 days, followed by 14 and 60.

How we built it

From free text to structured data

Storage sections are written for people. Temperatures show up in both units, in different orders, split across sentences, and sometimes mangled by OCR. Here is how we turned them into rows you can query.

01 · Read
54,664

current DailyMed SPL labels parsed for How Supplied / Storage and Handling text.

02 · Extract
Regex + LLM

Deterministic patterns handle the simple labels (about 30%). A structured LLM pass handles the rest.

03 · Verify
Every value

Each temperature is checked against a verbatim quote from the label. Failures go through an automatic repair loop.

04 · Ship
0

labels left in the human-review queue as of September 2026. Units are normalized, °C is the source of truth, and °F is included.

The full dataset

This page covers the trends. The dataset has one row per NDC.

The charts above are aggregates. The dataset gives you storage requirements for each product, keyed by NDC, ready to join to your formulary, inventory or claims.

  • ~177,000 NDC × ingredient rows, joinable on ndc11
  • Normalized primary_condition: room temperature, refrigerated, frozen or mixed
  • min/max temperature in °C and °F, with open-ended limits handled correctly
  • Every temperature requirement by stage (unopened, opened, excursion), not only the main one
  • Structured handling instructions and warnings: do not freeze, protect from light, discard after…
  • A verbatim source quote and a DailyMed link for every row
  • Full snapshots on a regular schedule, delivered as flat files you can load anywhere

Methodology

Source: current FDA Structured Product Labeling documents on DailyMed, September 2026 extract (54,664 labels). Unless noted, percentages are shares of the 42,711 labels that state a usable numeric temperature, and are rounded.

“Primary” range means the main storage requirement on each label. Excursion-only and in-use ranges are used only when a label gives nothing else. Trend groups use the effective date of each label's current version, not the product's approval date.

This page summarizes aggregate patterns. It is not clinical guidance. The FDA label is the authoritative source.
